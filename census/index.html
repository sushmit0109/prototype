Did development predict the vote?

A sub-district companion to the e-GP project's district-level "does winning pay?" study — but run in the opposite direction. The 2022 census is the last one taken, entirely before this election, so there's no post-election wave to test whether winning changed development. What the data can test instead: did the development conditions a decade of rule left behind predict which party an upazila's voters turned to once given a competitive choice.

The design, in short

A composite development index — literacy (15+), electricity access, safe sanitation, pucca (permanent) housing, and internet use, each z-scored and averaged — built from BBS's 2022 Population & Housing Census at upazila level, regressed against each party's Feb 2026 vote share with division fixed effects, a log(registered voters) control, and standard errors clustered by district. 449 of 542 election-side upazilas matched cleanly against the census's 507 upazila-level places (name variants resolved automatically; the six city-corporation metro areas — Dhaka, Chattogram, Cumilla, Barishal, Khulna, Rajshahi — are excluded outright, since the public 2022 tables report those as one aggregate unit each rather than by the individual thana the election data uses).

This is not the e-GP study's kind of causal

The district-level spending study earns "causal" by comparing the same districts to themselves before and after a real event, with a placebo period as the falsification check. Nothing here does that: one census wave, one election, no way to construct a placebo the way a panel allows. Division fixed effects and a population-size control are covariate adjustment, not identification — an omitted regional-political-culture variable, a historic party stronghold, or simple reverse causality (an area's politics shaping how services got delivered to it, not the other way round) could produce the same pattern this data would show. Read every coefficient below as an association net of those controls, not a proven effect.

Full method & data notes

Join: election_2026_unions.json (5,034 unions, netra.news / Election Commission publications, union-level with its own upazila field) aggregated up to upazila by summing votes, matched against the 2022 census's 507 upazila-level places by district+name (case/whitespace-insensitive, plus a handful of known spelling aliases — "Roypur"/"Raipur", "Kotwali"/"Jashore Sadar", and so on). Of — election-side upazilas: — excluded as metro city-corporation areas (no thana-level census breakdown exists publicly), — dropped for a missing value on one of the five core indicators (mostly electricity-access, which BBS didn't tabulate for every upazila), — genuinely unmatched.

Why 2022, not 2011→2022 change: the two censuses use incompatible table structures for the indicators that matter here — 2011 measures literacy at the household-head level, 2022 at the whole population; 2011 doesn't tabulate internet use or mobile banking at all. Comparing constructed "changes" between two different measurement definitions is a worse trap than using only the more recent, richer snapshot and saying plainly that it's a level, not a change.

Regression: OLS, y = a + b·x + division dummies + c·log(registered voters) + e, x z-scored per indicator, cluster-robust (CR1 sandwich, small-sample corrected) standard errors by district, t-distribution p-values. No panel dimension, no placebo test is possible in a single cross-section — the closest substitute for a robustness check here is that five different, independently-measured indicators (plus their composite) mostly agree with each other on finding nothing, rather than one convenient indicator being the only thing tested.

Sources: election results from netra.news's interactive results map (Nazmul Ahasan & Aaqib Md. Shatil), vote counts from Election Commission publications; census indicators from BBS's 2022 Population & Housing Census, parsed and curated in the companion census project.

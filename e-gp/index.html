Why it matters

A national total hides which parts of the country are actually being built up versus which are being passed over. Filtering by era also surfaces whether a change of government visibly redirected where money flows, or left the geography untouched while the rest of procurement behaviour shifted.

What it isn't

District of delivery, not district of the winning company — a Dhaka-based firm building a bridge in Bhola shows up under Bhola. Boundaries are simplified for legibility, not survey-accurate, and the newest district reclassifications may not all be reflected. The per-district sector mix (click a district) is procurement nature — Works/Goods/Services — not the finer CPV categories used in Sectors below; the tender search has no location filter, so CPV can't be attributed to a district without a much larger crawl.

Why it matters

Procurement behaviour is usually described as one long national trend line. Splitting it by government tests whether that's true, or whether it's really several distinct regimes stitched together — each with its own pace, its own competitiveness, and its own relationship to the estimate it started from.

What it isn't

The three eras are wildly different lengths — roughly 15 years, 18 months, and (as of this run) a few months. Rates (per day) make them comparable on pace; shares make them comparable on mix. But the elected-government column is the newest, smallest, and most likely to move as more of its own data accumulates — read it as early, not final.

A specific hypothesis, tested

—

The hypothesis: districts that did not elect a BNP-led-alliance seat majority in February 2026 are underrepresented in e-GP development spending. Since a district is really several constituencies (some districts gave BNP every seat, some none — see the scatter below), this is tested three ways: a binary seat-majority split, the district's continuous vote share, and its continuous seat share, not just whichever one looks most convenient. A raw comparison of "BNP districts vs. the rest" would confuse cause with a hundred things that make a district both likely to vote BNP and likely to receive money for unrelated reasons — so this runs a panel difference-in-differences instead: district and time-period fixed effects absorb everything time-invariant about a district and everything that moved every district together, leaving only whether a district's own spending moved differently, specifically in the period it went BNP, relative to districts that didn't.

That test has one make-or-break check: run the identical regression as if the "treatment" had happened at a moment when nobody's seats had actually been won yet. If a gap opens up in that fake period too, the real result can't be trusted as caused by the election. Getting that placebo period right turned out to matter enormously here — see "Full method" below for a design this analysis got wrong once, and the reasoning that fixed it.

Why this design

This is the difference between a correlation and a causal-shaped test, made concrete: a naive comparison here would have reported exactly what the hypothesis predicts (the plain group-mean gap is positive, in the "BNP districts get more" direction). Once the panel is confined to periods where e-GP's own platform coverage is stable — see "Full method" for why that confinement is necessary — that gap is not statistically distinguishable from zero on the three smooth treatment definitions, and neither is its own placebo. A real effect that a correlation-only analysis would have reported with confidence does not survive being tested properly. The "some vs. none" split-districts comparison directly above looked like the one exception at first — clearing conventional significance on the real test and not on either placebo — until a division-level control made the p-value collapse too; see the note alongside it for the full account of why that's reported as explained-away, not as a finding.

What this isn't

Not proof that no favouritism exists anywhere, in any form — this tests specific, falsifiable versions of the hypothesis (seat-level election outcome → district-level e-GP spending, in the months since the interim government made e-GP use obligatory) and nearly all of them find nothing. It doesn't rule out an effect too small or too recent to detect, a mechanism that doesn't run through e-GP procurement specifically, or favouritism operating below the district level (a party rewarding specific constituencies or unions within a district it also lost, say) that a district-level test can't see at all. The post-election window is, as of this build, only a few months. And spending is only known to district level; the underlying election results go all the way to union level (5,034 of them) and are published in full in the repository, but e-GP contracts don't carry union or upazila detail, so the causal test itself is necessarily coarser than the election data feeding it.

A different kind of favour: same money, fewer vendors?

Winning could pay off without a district's total envelope changing at all, if the same money started going to a narrower set of contractors instead. This runs the identical district+month regression, real test and both placebos, with a district-month's vendor-concentration index (HHI — the sum of each vendor's squared share of that month's contract value; 1.0 would mean one vendor took everything) as the outcome instead of taka per voter.

Findings

Eleven specific claims, each closed until you open it

Finding 01 — Timing

One in every seven contracts is signed in June — the last month of the fiscal year. In August, it's one in twenty-two.

Bangladesh's fiscal year ends 30 June. Contract signings cluster hard against that deadline and collapse immediately after it, the classic signature of year-end budget flush: money that must be committed before it lapses.

Why it matters

Procurement rushed to beat a deadline gets less scrutiny, fewer bidders, and weaker price discipline than the same purchase made in October. The spike is a standing indicator of where quality risk concentrates.

What it isn't

Not evidence of wrongdoing. Some seasonality is legitimate — construction avoids the monsoon, and annual supply contracts naturally renew on the fiscal calendar.

Finding 02 — Competition

In 2015, 94% of contract value went through fully open tendering. It collapsed to 66% in 2019 and has never returned to its old level.

Open Tendering (OTM) is the default competitive route: anyone qualified may bid. The value that left it went to limited, invitation-only tendering (LTM) and to two-envelope variants (OSTETM) — both narrower processes. The share has partly rebounded since, ranging from 70% to 80%, but it has not been near 90% in any year since 2017.

Why it matters

Openness is the single strongest structural defence against both overpricing and favouritism. A 20-point drop in the share of money exposed to open competition is a material change in how the state buys.

What it isn't

Limited tendering is legal and appropriate for small or urgent purchases. The chart shows a shift in mix, not a count of violations.

Finding 03 — Plan vs outcome

— of contracts are awarded at almost exactly 10% below the government's own estimate. Another — land on exactly 5% below.

The Annual Procurement Plan records, before any bidding happens, what each package should cost. Competitive bidding against that estimate would produce a smooth spread of outcomes. What the data shows instead is two narrow spikes on round discount rates — the signature of a discount applied by convention rather than discovered by competition.

The other tail: when the award comes in over estimate

— packages were awarded above their own estimate by more than 15% — — in total, above what was already budgeted for them. That's a small share of the matched packages, and a large individual overrun can be a legitimate re-scope rather than a red flag — but it's worth naming which ones they are.

Why it matters

A price that lands on a fixed discount off a published estimate is a price nobody competed for. Neither that nor an overrun is visible unless you hold the plan and the award side by side — which is what the itemised procurement plan makes possible for the first time here.

Coverage & caveats

—

A conventional discount is not itself illegal, and some agencies may set ceilings this way deliberately. The finding is that the number is being set, not that a rule was broken. A large overrun can also be a legitimate re-scope, added quantity, or a stale estimate — each row is a lead, not a verdict.

Finding 04 — Cost structure

The size of the "discount" off the government's own estimate isn't one national number — it's a habit that differs by ministry, and it has a visible start date.

This dataset has no independent, external cost benchmark to test estimates against — nothing here can say a price is objectively too high. What it can test is whether the typical gap between the government's own estimate and the award behaves like ordinary estimation error (roughly the same everywhere) or like a locally-set convention. It's the latter: the typical award sits anywhere from 1-2% below estimate to over 16% below, depending which ministry bought it.

And nationally, that convention has a start date

Share of matched packages landing within half a point of exactly 10% below estimate, by year of signing. Before 2015 this pattern barely exists; it becomes routine from the late 2010s on.

Why it matters

A ministry running a consistent 16% haircut off its own estimates and one running barely 1-2% are not making the same kind of estimate, or bidding into the same kind of market — yet both get reported the same way. And the national convention appearing from the late 2010s onward, rather than always having been there, suggests it was adopted rather than discovered: something to trace back to a specific circular or practice, not an eternal feature of how estimates work.

What it isn't

Not a claim that any ministry's prices are "wrong" against some outside standard — no such standard is in this data, only each ministry's own estimate. Ministries below the 30-matched-package floor aren't shown, and even the ones that qualify range from dozens of matched packages to tens of thousands — read the hover text for each bar's actual count before weighing it against its neighbours.

Finding 05 — Threshold effects

Contract values bunch just under ৳50 crore and fall off sharply just above it — the shape a ceiling gets gamed into, not the shape a market prices into.

Packages above roughly this size enter a slower, higher-visibility approval chain; below it, an office can approve on its own. — as many contracts land just under ৳50 crore as land just over it.

The same office, the same vendor, several awards, one clear pattern

— office–vendor pairs show two or more contracts, each individually under ৳50 crore, signed within 45 days of each other, that together clear it. A single genuine need does not usually arrive as several near-simultaneous small ones.

Why it matters

Splitting one job into several sub-threshold packages is a well-known way to keep a procurement inside the fast, low-scrutiny lane. Both tests here are indirect — value bunching and time-clustered awards — but they point the same direction, and a real instance of splitting would leave exactly this signature.

What it isn't

Neither test proves splitting on its own. A vendor can legitimately win several separate small jobs from the same office in the same season — a regional supplier doing routine repeat work. Each row here is a lead worth a specific look, not a finding of wrongdoing. Bunching is measured on a modest sample (a few hundred contracts near this value out of 871,684 total), so treat the ratio as suggestive, not precise.

Concentration has innocent causes: sole regional suppliers, one multi-year project split into many contracts, or genuinely specialised goods. Each row needs its own look.

Finding 07 — Pricing

One in five contracts is priced at one of just twenty values — and those cluster far more heavily on invitation-only awards than contracts generally do.

Tk 9,50,000 alone appears — times, roughly eight times its neighbouring prices. Across the top twenty repeated prices, — of contracts were awarded by limited tendering, against a — baseline across all contracts. The single most repeated prices run as high as 93% limited-tender.

Why it matters

Identical prices recurring thousands of times across unrelated offices suggests a price being set to a convention rather than discovered by competition — and the strong tilt toward invitation-only awards is consistent with that.

What it isn't

The portal quotes value in crore to three decimals, so all values snap to Tk 10,000 steps. That rounding is uniform and cannot manufacture a spike — but it does mean each "price" is really a Tk 10,000-wide band.

Why it matters

A single fast-growing firm might just be a well-run business. A cluster of otherwise unremarkable companies that happen to share a beneficial owner, all trending up at once, is a different shape entirely — the kind of pattern that only becomes visible once ownership and growth are read together rather than as separate tables. High single-office concentration (see the "Concentration" column) on a company that appeared only recently is the narrowest version of that same shape: one relationship, not a market.

What it isn't

Fast, organic growth is normal and common — most names here are probably exactly that, not a red flag. A joint venture legitimately shares "ownership" with its parent companies, which is why some owner groups below list a JV alongside the firms that formed it; that's a real corporate structure, not evidence of anything. Ratios are floored (a real baseline footprint must already exist) to avoid the near-zero-base problem that inflates a ratio into nonsense, and ownership coverage is the same sampled ~20,000-vendor set as the Suppliers finding above — a company outside that sample simply won't show a shared-owner flag even if one exists. Every row is a lead worth a specific look, not a proven finding.

The mix is shifting, era by era

Why it matters

A national procurement dataset that's read as one lump number obscures this entirely. Split by what's actually being bought, the picture is: Bangladesh's e-GP system is overwhelmingly a construction-procurement system that happens to also handle everything else, not a general-purpose one where sectors compete for comparable shares of activity — though that gap has been narrowing, not by construction volume collapsing so much as everything else growing around it.

What it isn't

A tender can carry more than one CPV category, so shares are of all tenders, not of each other — they don't sum to 100%. This is a count of tenders, not a share of money: a handful of large construction contracts and a much larger number of small ICT purchases would still show up as "construction dominates" here. This is the top-level category only (each has its own subtree of finer detail, not fetched here), and the era split reflects when a tender was published, not when it was awarded or completed.

Methodology

How this was built, and where it can be wrong

The false-positive that nearly shipped

Matching contract winners against the debarment register on company name alone produced 8,707 "active violation" flags worth ৳53.4 billion. It was wrong. The largest contributor, khan enterprise, accounted for 637 of them: "Khan" is among the most common surnames in Bangladesh and "Enterprise" a generic suffix. That name appears on 1,984 contracts across 15+ districts, while the register holds exactly one such firm at one Dhaka address — dozens of unrelated small businesses, not one company evading a ban.

Flags now require the contract's district to match the debarred firm's registered address, and exclude any name appearing as a winner in more than five districts nationwide. That cut 8,707 to 246. The filter deliberately trades missed real violations for fewer false accusations.

A finding this page briefly published and then retracted

An earlier version of Finding 03 reported that a procurement package becomes less open than planned about 10 times as often as it becomes more open (568 vs 59), joining the itemised procurement plan to awarded contracts on package reference text. The join had the same flaw as the debarment case above: some offices type a bare running number ("01", "280"), office shorthand ("se", "ee"), or even the literal placeholder "na" into that field instead of a real package code. Those aren't unique, so the join was pairing a plan estimate with whichever unrelated contract happened to share the same short text — one single "na"-to-"na" collision alone produced 16 of the original 568. After excluding references used by more than three procuring entities nationally, references under six characters, and matches at an implausible ratio (a real estimate-to-award relationship does not plausibly differ by two orders of magnitude), the true count is 19 less-open vs. 18 more-open — no detectable direction at this sample size. The chart and claim have been removed rather than kept with a smaller number, since there's nothing left to show. The same fix changed the >115%-overrun figures in this finding and the ministry breakdown in Finding 04 nearby: an earlier pass at those showed sharp differences between ministries (Finance and Education standing out as high-overrun) that turned out to be the identical artifact, not a real pattern — see `GENERIC_PE_SPREAD`, `MIN_REF_LEN`, and `SANITY_RATIO_MIN`/`MAX` in `build_plan_vs_actual.py`.

Derived vs. reported — what the source never actually said
  • Debarment severity is computed here (scope breadth × duration × repeat offences). The register publishes no rating.
  • Reason categories are keyword-tagged from free text written by whichever office filed the debarment.
  • "Mostly buys" joins procurement nature from the tender list, which matches only 53% of contracts — thinner before 2018.
  • Concentration is tested only on offices with 50+ contracts and 50+ crore in spend; below that, one big contract looks like a monopoly.

Where the district map geometry comes from

District boundaries are not from eprocure.gov.bd — nothing there publishes shapes. They're traced from nuhil/bangladesh-geocode's district-level GeoJSON, itself derived from the standard UN OCHA/HDX Bangladesh administrative boundary set, simplified from ~44,000 to ~11,700 coordinate points for page weight (see build_district_geo.py) and projected with a plain equirectangular transform corrected for Bangladesh's latitude. It's a one-off build step, not re-run daily — only the spend/count data underneath it updates. Sixty-nine district spellings across pre- and post-2018 renamings (Chittagong/Chattogram, Comilla/Cumilla, and three more) collapse onto the 64 real districts in districts.py.

Ownership matching

Award detail pages carry a Tenderer ID and a beneficial-ownership table, but only on recent awards — a mid-2024 award page has neither field; an August 2026 one has both, tracking a 2025-era rules change. Rather than fetch all 871,684 contract pages for data that doesn't vary per contract, one representative (most recent) contract is sampled per vendor, largest vendors first.

Owner names are matched only when the normalised name has three or more parts, which reduces but does not remove collisions. Every group needs a human check that the companies form a real corporate family before it means anything.

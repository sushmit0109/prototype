The Persistence of Form

Bangladesh has rewritten its constitutional order four times since 1799. This study asks what that turnover did to the form of its law — and finds: remarkably little. Twenty-five analyses over all 1,652 statutory instruments, measured natively in both English and Bengali.

What changes
Democratic parliaments legislate by Act (0.1% decree); military and interim rule by decree (62.8%). Ordinances carry 12.5× the adjusted odds of repeal.
What does not
Must appears in 7.5% of laws against shall in 98.2%. Drafting quality, trope density and motif density are flat across every era.
What is rising
Supremacy clauses climb for 170 years across the language switch (τ=0.73, p=0.0002). Legislatures enact around inconvenient law rather than reform it.

F3

Post-Uprising 2024+ Ordinances

The 2026 parliament's verdict on all 133 interim-government ordinances

The interim government installed after the July 2024 popular uprising promulgated 133 ordinances before the elected parliament convened in 2026. A special parliamentary committee (chaired by BNP's Joynal Abedin) reviewed all 133 against a constitutional April 10, 2026 deadline. 113 confirmed as Acts — 98 as-is, 15 passed with amendments. 4 formally repealed (বাতিল/রহিত) by parliamentary vote: the Parliament Secretariat Ordinance 2024 and the three Supreme Court judiciary-independence ordinances (Judge Appointment; Court Secretariat; its 2026 Amendment). 16 lapsed automatically at the April 10 deadline — parliament did not place them as Bills, so they expired under Art. 93 of the Constitution. The 16 include the Referendum Ordinance, Enforced Disappearance Prevention Ordinance, National Human Rights Commission Ordinances (2024–25), Revenue Policy Ordinances, ACC Amendment, Civil Aviation Amendment, Travel Agency Amendment, Microfinance Bank Ordinance, and Right to Information Amendment. Part G

Legislative Drafting Linguistics

G1

Modal Verb Ecology

shall / may / must per 1,000 words by decade — English corpus

Legal language is organised around obligation. The three modal verbs encode different legal weights: 'shall' (mandatory duty), 'may' (permissive authority), 'must' (modern mandatory, avoiding 'shall' ambiguity). Bangladesh's statute book is heavily shall-dominated across all eras — averaging 11.9 'shall' per 1,000 words in English laws and 19.3 in Bengali laws. The UK and Australian plain-language movements (2000s) actively replaced 'shall' with 'must', but Bangladesh's drafting culture never adopted this reform. 'Must' remains near-zero across the corpus — Bangladesh legislators have yet to make the distinction. The 1990s dip in 'shall' coincides with the democratic transition and a wave of procedural laws that favour 'may'-phrased empowerment clauses over mandatory duty-imposition. Theoretical grounding: Garner (2001) Legal Writing in Plain English; Tiersma (1999) Legal Language.

G2

Archaic Legal Vocabulary Index

Colonial-era legal fossils persist across 220 years — and Bengali laws are MORE archaic than English

Archaic legal vocabulary — herein, aforesaid, thereof, whatsoever, deem to be, pursuant to (English) and উক্ত, তদ্কর্তৃক, অতঃপর, তদনুযায়ী (Bengali) — is counted per 1,000 words for each era. The headline finding: Bengali-language laws (gold) show significantly higher archaism (11.4/1k) than their English counterparts (4.6/1k). Rather than modernising the drafting style, post-1987 Bengali drafters transplanted the colonial register wholesale into the new script. The Post-Uprising era shows the lowest English archaism (2.9/1k), suggesting some plain-language improvement in recent drafting. However, Post-Uprising Bengali laws spike to 14.8/1k — the highest of any era — possibly because many were drafted by officials trained in older traditions. Grounding: Tiersma (1999); Crystal & Davy (1969) Investigating English Style; Cao (2007) Translating Law on how translation amplifies rather than reduces archaic register.

G3

Conditional Clause Density by Policy Domain

Land & Property and Local Government laws are the most conditionally complex

Conditional clauses — if, unless, subject to, notwithstanding, provided that, save where — are the primary mechanism through which legislation creates exceptions, qualifications, and dependencies. Counted per 1,000 words across all laws in each policy domain. Land & Property (8.3/1k) and Local Government (8.2/1k) lead all domains — reflecting the exceptionally complex property-rights and administrative-boundary structures these laws regulate. Criminal Law & Justice (7.5/1k) is third — its conditional density reflects the elaborate statutory carve-outs around jurisdiction, mens rea, and defences. Finance, Banking & Tax (4.1/1k) is lowest, perhaps counterintuitively — much fiscal complexity is handled through subsidiary rules and schedules rather than inline conditionals. Grounding: Bhatia (1993) Analysing Genre on conditional structure as a defining feature of legislative texts.

G4

Nominalization Density (English Statutes)

Abstract nouns (-tion/-ment/-ance/-ity) as a legal-genre marker across 220 years

Nominalization — converting verb actions into abstract nouns — is a signature of formal legal register: 'determine' becomes 'determination', 'enforce' becomes 'enforcement', 'eligible' becomes 'eligibility'. Bhatia (1993) identified nominalization as one of the three defining macro-features of Commonwealth legislative genre (alongside post-modification and passive voice). The nominalization index counts all words with abstract-noun suffixes (-tion/-sion/-ment/-ance/-ence/-ity/-ness/-ure/-ship) per 1,000 words. The metric runs at 60–110/1k throughout the corpus — roughly one nominalized form for every 10–15 words. Colonial-era laws (1850–1950s) peak highest, consistent with Victorian administrative language. Modern laws (2009–24) show a modest decline, but the values remain far above general-prose norms (~30–40/1k), confirming that no plain-language reform has displaced nominalization as Bangladesh's default drafting mode.

G5

Passive Voice Density by Era

Agency concealment through the passive voice — sustained across 220 years

Legal drafting is famously passive: 'the application shall be submitted' rather than 'you must submit'. The passive voice removes the subject-agent from view, creating apparent objectivity and avoiding direct address. Passive constructions are counted per 1,000 words for both English and Bengali corpora across eras. The English corpus averages 16.4 passive constructions per 1,000 words — approximately one passive every 3–4 sentences. Early Bangladesh (1971–90) English laws show the highest density, coinciding with the large volume of presidential orders and martial-law ordinances from that era, which are characteristically impersonal in tone. Bengali-language laws also show high passive density, which maps onto the Bengali legal passive pattern করা হইবে / দেওয়া হবে (literally 'will be done/given'). The Post-Uprising era shows some reduction, consistent with a reported shift toward more direct administrative drafting. Grounding: Maley (1994) 'The language of legislation'; Tiersma (1999) on passive as a legal-register feature.

G6

Chained Proviso Intensity by Policy Domain

1,179 'provided further that' chains across the corpus — where do they concentrate?

A 'provided further that' (or 'provided also that') clause is a qualification layered onto an already-qualified provision. The UK Renton Committee (1975) The Preparation of Legislation identified chained provisos as a primary source of statutory obscurity in Commonwealth drafting — yet Bangladesh's statute book contains 1,179 such chains across 2,305 laws. This chart shows the average number of chained provisos per law within each policy domain. Finance, Banking & Tax and Education & Research laws carry the most chains per law, reflecting the dense exception-structures those domains require. Chained provisos appear across all eras: the colonial period's 109, the Pakistan era's 89, Early Bangladesh's 218, Democratic era's 278, and Modern era's 410 — rising in absolute terms as the statute book grows, with no sign of drafting-reform reduction. Grounding: Maley (1994); Bhatia (1993); Renton Committee (1975); Driedger (1982) The Construction of Statutes on the proviso as a drafting pathology.

Part H

Amendment Journeys

H1

Amendment Journeys

How 12 landmark laws changed across political regimes — three distinct patterns of legislative evolution

Hover any marker to see the political driver

Each row is a law or legal lineage; each dot is an amendment or replacement event. Circle = Parliamentary Act · Diamond = Executive Ordinance · Square = Presidential Order. Dot colour shows which government enacted the change; background shading marks political eras. Three patterns are illustrated: (1) incremental amendment — successive changes to a single Act; (2) reform reversal — a reform undone by the next government; (3) successive repeal chain — each law repealing and replacing its predecessor.

Cyber Law Lineage (2018→2026) — successive repeal chain: Digital Security Act 2018 (DSA) used to prosecute 1,000+ journalists and critics; 'repealed' by the cosmetically renamed Cyber Security Act 2023 (CSA) with no substantive change; genuinely replaced post-uprising by the Cyber Safety Ordinance 2024 (CSO), which removed 'offensive content' offences entirely; two further amendments refined the CSO; BNP parliament converted it to permanent law in 2026, preserving the text verbatim.

National Human Rights Commission Act (2009) — reform reversal: AL enacted NHRC with executive-controlled commissioners; post-uprising interim government reformed it (2024) giving the Commission genuine independence and self-initiated investigation powers; BNP parliamentary majority reversed those reforms in 2026, reinstating executive appointment before the BNP's own governance record could face independent NHRC scrutiny.

Bangladesh Telecommunication Regulation Act (2001): enacted under BNP to liberalise telecoms; amended in 2006 to add surveillance powers; Awami League used Ordinance (2009) and Act (2010) for 3G rollout; post-uprising Ordinance (2025) repealed internet-shutdown clauses; 2026 parliament added net-neutrality and 5G framework.

Int'l Crimes (Tribunals) Act (1973): Mujib enacted for 1971 war crimes, trials incomplete before his 1975 assassination; dormant 50 years; four post-uprising Ordinances (2024–25) activated and expanded the tribunal to try Hasina-regime officials for the 2024 student massacre.

Upazila Parishad Act (1998): AL decentralisation vision; 2022 restoration kept MP budget-control, undermining autonomy; post-uprising Ordinances (2024–25) removed that clause.

Representation of the People Order (1972): independence-era election rules; AL 2023 amendment accused of entrenchment; post-uprising reformed delimitation and commission independence.

BERC Act (2003): BNP market reform; AL amendments progressively weakened regulator independence, culminating in the 2023 'secret LNG' clause; reversed by 2024 post-uprising Ordinance.

Code of Criminal Procedure (1898): 127-year-old British colonial code; three instruments in 18 months (2025–26) for democratic reform and digital-era criminal procedure.

Money Laundering Prevention Act (2002) — successive repeal chain: BNP's 2002 MLPA met FATF minimum standards; AL replaced it wholesale with the Anti-Money Laundering Act 2009 (broader predicate offences, heavier penalties); updated again in 2012 to comply with FATF's mutual evaluation action plan.

Anti-Corruption Commission Act (2004) — reform reversal: BNP established independent ACC in 2004; AL's c.2013 amendment required prior parliamentary clearance before prosecuting government officials — effectively shielding ruling-party allies; post-uprising 2025 Ordinance removed the clearance requirement and restored ACC independence.

Income Tax Law (1984 → 2023) — generational replacement: Ershad's 1984 military ordinance served as Bangladesh's tax code for 40 years, amended 60+ times without ever being replaced; AL's Income Tax Act 2023 finally consolidated all amendments into a new democratic Act; post-uprising Ordinance 2025 removed the 'black money whitening' amnesty clause.

VAT Law (1991 → 2012) — successive repeal + 7-year implementation delay: BNP's 1991 VAT Act introduced Bangladesh's first VAT; AL's comprehensive 2012 replacement was postponed five times over seven years before finally taking effect in 2019 — reflecting sustained business-community resistance to the new compliance regime; post-uprising 2025 Ordinances removed industry exemptions and extended VAT to foreign digital platforms.

P1

Across the Language Switch

Measured natively in each script

Machine translation was rejected: it normalises exactly the register features this study measures. Instead each script is measured on its own terms. Supremacy claims and delegation both rise continuously through the 1987 transition — the trend does not break where the language does.

P2

Bengali Legal Register

সাধু vs চলিত — invisible to translation

English legal archaism is lexical; Bengali archaism is grammatical. Over a third of paired forms in 2020s legislation remain in the literary সাধু register that ordinary written Bengali abandoned mid-century. The 1987 Act changed the language of law without modernising its register.

Part S

Penalty Erosion

S1

Penalty Erosion

What a fine was worth, and what it is worth

The median penalty enacted in the 1970s was set at 5,000 Taka — worth about 70,600 Taka today — and is still 5,000 Taka. A loss of 96.2%. Nothing in Bangladeshi law revalorises statutory monetary amounts.

S2

The Decay Curve

Penalties that no longer deter

Bars = share of that decade's penalties whose maximum is now below one day's minimum wage (~500 Tk). Line = median loss of real value. 13.2% of 1970s penalties have fallen below that line — seven such laws are still Active, including the Pharmacy Ordinance 1976, whose maximum fine remains 105 Taka. The 1990s, 2000s and 2010s bars are genuinely zero, not missing data: those decades set penalties in much larger nominal sums (median maximum 17,500 → 100,000 → 200,000 Tk), so even after losing 79%, 71% and 42% of their value respectively, none has yet decayed below a day's wage. The lone 2020s case is a single 2023 statute that fixed a 200 Tk maximum. Read the two series together: the line shows every cohort is eroding; the bars show only the oldest have eroded far enough to stop deterring.
